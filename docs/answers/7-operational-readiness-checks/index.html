7 Ways to Test Operational Readiness Beyond Training Completion

Seven practical readiness checks for real-world performance, decisions, friction, communication and observable evidence.

By Ed Reif · 2026-11-16 · An educational framework; not a claim of independently validated results.

Completion proves that an activity was completed; it does not, by itself, prove capability in changed conditions. These seven checks translate the Ed Reif Field Toolkit into practical review questions.

1. Define the mission task

What must a person actually accomplish, under what conditions, and to what standard? Replace “knows the procedure” with an observable performance statement.

2. Test whether the operator detects changed conditions

Introduce a credible anomaly or conflicting signal. Can the person separate new information from noise and explain why a response is necessary?

3. Apply purposeful friction

Use realistic constraints such as an information delay, degraded communications or competing priorities. Friction is diagnostic when it resembles conditions in which capability matters.

4. Require a defensible decision

Ask the performer to choose, explain the governing limit, and escalate or stop where appropriate. Avoid treating confident talk as evidence of sound judgment.

5. Observe the communication handoff

Did the intended meaning arrive, was it understood and was the agreed action performed? A sent message is not the same as a verified handoff.

6. Evaluate recovery and safe stopping

A capable person can recognize when to intervene, request support or decline to continue. Assess justified actions, not only whether a scenario ends successfully.

7. Change the conditions and retest

Record the gap, update practice or support, and assess again in a modified scenario. Repeatable evidence is stronger than an unqualified success label.

What is the evidence?

Record task, conditions, signal recognition, choice, limit, timing, communications, intervention and remaining gap. This is a methodology, not a statistical study or a claim of demonstrated field results. Continue with the scenario-design checklist.
